zSwap: the first exchange that runs 100% on the blockchain

The page you trade on is stored in eleven contracts. zSwap's HTML lives on Ethereum mainnet as contract code, so there is no server in the path and no file to pin. The bytes at an address can never be edited, including by us.

A normal front end reaches you over a domain, from a host, through a build pipeline, and any of those can be taken over without touching the contracts. There is nothing here to take over. The page is bytecode at an address, a gateway can only show it to you, and anyone can read the same bytes straight from the chain. Nothing is custodial either: orders stay cancellable and liquidity withdrawable, by you alone.

Trading

A swap, end to end

Everything below happens between typing an amount and signing once. The page quotes every venue it can reach, plans a route across them, and settles it in a single transaction.

01Quote. zQuoter prices the trade across Uniswap V2, V3 and V4, Sushi, Curve, Lido, zAMM and the Precision pools. The orderbooks are read in the same breath, and every read is pinned to one block, so the comparison is of a single moment.
02Plan. The best single venue is the baseline. Where resting orders beat it on part of the size, the page composes a split and re-quotes the AMM against the remainder. A mixed route then has to earn its extra gas. The extra legs are costed at roughly 120,000 gas plus 105,000 per order, converted into the same token the saving is measured in, and the split wins only if the saving beats that cost by 20%. If that conversion cannot be priced, the fallback is a flat margin: 5 bps, plus 2 more for every order the route fills.
03Settle. zRouter executes the whole plan in one transaction, on one signature, with the output minimum enforced on the balance that actually arrives.

What you see before signing

  • Slippage is the worst price you'll accept. Past it, the trade reverts instead of filling badly.
  • Price impact is how far your own size moves the price. The page warns as it grows and won't quietly let a terrible one through.
  • Min received or Max paid, the venue that won, and the effective rate.
  • Every search here runs under a bound, and the rate line says so when one bites: Book scan capped when order discovery stopped early, Planner heuristic/capped when the route planner hit its candidate or leg limits, Precision scan capped (128) when the market scan did. Each one means a better combination may exist past what was searched. AMM only (books offline) means the boards could not be read at all, and Split — bound widened means a split route needed a wider slippage bound than you set, with the new figure printed.

Quoting: zQuoter

Most venues are priced with local math. V4 cannot always be, so it gets its own machinery.

zQuoterV4Ordinary V4 pools. Stays a pure view by reading through StateView, and matches Uniswap's canonical quoter exactly, protocol fees included.
V4QuoteLensPools with a hook. A hook can replace the curve, take a cut of the output, or set its own fee per swap, none of which is visible in the pool's stored state. So this one quotes by running the hook for real, through PoolManager.unlock.
V4PortExecutes any V4 pool in either direction.

Settling: zRouter

zRouter speaks those venues natively through a compact ABI, and reaches anything else through snwap: input tokens go to a venue-specific executor, the executor is called, and the router measures the recipient's balance before and after. The minimum is enforced on that measured difference, so a venue that misreports its own fill still cannot underpay you. The call itself runs through SafeExecutor, which holds no token approvals, so reaching an unknown venue can never become a call on the router's allowances.

Several router calls can share a transaction through multicall, which delegatecalls back into the router. Delegatecall preserves msg.value, so every entry in a batch sees the same ether. That is a constraint worth knowing rather than a feature: an ether route cannot be split across sibling entries, which is why a plan that is part ask, part bid and part AMM is composed inside one executor call instead, by Swapbol below.

ETH and WETH are interchangeable at the interface, with the router wrapping or unwrapping whichever way the venue needs in the same call. Deadlines revert as Expired, an ether amount that does not match the value sent as InvalidMsgVal, and a short fill as SnwapSlippage, which names the token, what arrived and what was required.

The boards do not fit that shape: they pull tokens from their caller and pay their caller, where snwap hands tokens to an executor first. Each therefore gets a small adapter rather than a special case inside the router.

OrderbolPlacing orders. Routes into Swapboard, Dutchboard and Floorboard. A checkpoint(token) in transient storage snapshots the balance immediately before funding, and it is single-use, consumed by the same caller in the same transaction, and cleared before the token or a board gets control. An order can therefore only be funded by the increase that arrived after the checkpoint, so tokens already sitting at the adapter can't be swept into somebody's new order.
SwapbolFilling orders. Ask fills and bid hits, using exact call-scoped allowances that are revoked before it returns. Both sides live in one adapter because a route that's part ask, part bid and part AMM only works inside a single executor call.

Resting liquidity

Orderbooks

The resting orders in step 02 live on three boards, each holding real escrow on chain. They are also worth using directly: instead of taking whatever the pool offers now, you can post the price you want and wait.

SwapboardA price you name. Either side can be an ERC-20 or an ERC-721. Your asset is escrowed when you create the order: released to the taker when it fills, or back to you when you cancel. Partial fills, an expiry and a named counterparty are each optional, and a WETH order can be cancelled straight back to native ETH. A taker signs a floor on what they receive, so repricing a live order before their transaction lands cannot make them take less than they agreed to.
DutchboardA price that falls. The total price decays from your start price to your floor over a window you choose, for ERC-20 lots and NFTs alike. Two things to know: each partial fill rounds its cost up on its own, so splitting a purchase can cost more than taking it in one go, and a listing with no expiry stays live at the floor until you cancel.
FloorboardThe bid side, and the mirror of Dutchboard. A climbing, partially fillable bid for an ERC-20 or a whole NFT collection. What is escrowed is the payment, sized at the end price, since that is the most the bid can ever owe. NFT orders on any board are shown and tagged here, but creating or filling one is a direct-wallet action against the board rather than something this page routes.
View lensesRead-only planners the page reads before it proposes a route: SwapboardView over both Swapboards and Dutchboard, FloorboardView over the bids. The former caps planning at 256 candidates, drops all-or-nothing rows too large for the current request before spending that cap, and bounds token metadata calls by gas and returndata size. A hostile symbol() comes back blank rather than breaking the read.

Every position is an NFT that draws itself

An order on any board is an ERC-721 whose tokenURI is a self-contained SVG card, rendered on-chain by an immutable contract the board deployed in its own constructor. Nothing is fetched at display time, so a card cannot go stale independently of the order it describes.

Sending, and SLOW sends

Not everything the page does is a trade. It also sends plain transfers, resolving 0x, .eth, .wei and .gwei recipients. A SLOW send is time-locked and reversible by you until it matures, which is worth using for large amounts and for addresses you have not paid before. A locked transfer does not deliver itself: tick auto-claim and the page attaches a small keeper tip, refunded if you reverse it.

Pools

Precision pools

The last venue in the quote is the one we build ourselves: a concentrated constant-product pool that is one contract per market, deployed by PrecisionPoolFactory at a CREATE2 address anyone can re-derive from the committed source.

The pair, the price band and the fee are immutables written in at deployment, much as a Uniswap V3 pool fixes its own pair and tier, so the saving is not in reading those. It is in what a market-specific contract never has to do: no tick bitmap, no pool-key hashing, no singleton flash accounting, no hook dispatch when there is no hook, and no WETH round trip on an ether pair. A swap inside the band is a single multiply-divide against reserves plus their virtual offsets, then the fee and the transfer. Only a swap large enough to reach the band edge does more work, bisecting to find how much of it fits.

The bandLiquidity is concentrated between two prices instead of spread along an infinite curve, so the same capital quotes tighter. A position earns while the price sits inside its band, and holds one side or the other at the edges. A swap that would push the price past an edge cannot simply be filled at a price the band does not support, so swapExactIn reverts. Use swapUpTo instead and the pool fills as much as fits, returning how much of your input it consumed. That entry point is unavailable on a hooked pool.
FeesA base fee set per market, from which the creator may take a share of at most half, plus a hook surcharge where a market has one. The total is capped at 10%. LP fees stay in the reserves rather than accruing separately, so the effective product grows above L² over time. Two things integrators must model: totalSupply is the liquidity the virtual offsets are built from and not sqrt(XY) of live reserves, and a position sitting at a boundary may still hold some of the other side, which is retained fees and belongs to the LPs.
Native etherEther pairs hold ETH itself. No wrap on the way in, no unwrap on the way out.
Hooks, optionalA market may name a hook for a bounded fee surcharge and a post-swap callback. The pool caps the surcharge itself and runs the callback under a gas limit, ignoring a failure rather than reverting, so a hook can price a trade but never trap one. Where no hook is named there is no dispatch at all.
If you integrate directly, check factory(). The pool constructor is public, so anyone can deploy a look-alike naming a factory of their own. It shares no state with a canonical market and cannot touch its reserves, but in a wallet prompt it looks the same. One call settles it: pool.factory() against the canonical factory, or factory.isPool(pool). This page never reads a raw pool address, going through PrecisionPoolLens and isPool instead, so a trader here is already covered. Anyone approving a pool address found elsewhere is not.

Around the factory sit PrecisionRoute, the zRouter executor that puts these markets in the ordinary quote path; PrecisionLauncher, which opens a market and seeds it in one transaction; and PrecisionPoolLens and PrecisionLiquidityLens, the swap and LP previews the page reads.

Opening a market costs around 4.63M gas, most of it the code deposit for the pool contract itself, so the page prices it before the wallet prompt rather than letting a default tip size a transaction this large.

Storage

A web page kept as bytecode

All of that is ordinary DeFi machinery. What is unusual is where the interface driving it lives. A contract's code is public and anyone can copy it out with EXTCODECOPY, so zSwap uses code as storage. The page is not a file the contract points at. The page is the contract, eleven times over.

01Slice the page. No single contract may exceed 24,576 bytes of code, which EIP-170 has capped since 2016. This build is 261,091 bytes, so it goes out as eleven slices of 23,736, each with 840 bytes to spare.
02Deploy each slice behind a tiny stub that runs once and returns the slice as the new contract's code. What lands on chain is page bytes and nothing else: no functions, no storage, nothing to call.
03Deploy the wrapper holding those eleven addresses as immutable pointers. Its constructor reverts unless all eleven are there, non-empty and distinct, so a half-assembled page cannot be deployed by mistake.
04Read it back. html() copies the slices into a single string in the order they were given. Any wallet, node or browser gateway can decode it as-is.

Code is far cheaper than storage for this. The same HTML in SSTORE slots would cost roughly 20k gas per 32-byte word to write, and reading it back would mean thousands of SLOADs rather than one copy per slice.

Permanence is the other half. No function on the wrapper can reach those pointers, so the answer to html() is fixed for the life of the chain. That is what lets the dapp send Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable and mean it: a cache may keep this page forever, because there is no version of the future in which it differs.

Reading it yourself

cast call <zswap-address> "html()(string)" --rpc-url <rpc> > zSwap.html

Versions

How an immutable dapp still gets fixed

So if the bytes can never change, how does anything ever get fixed? The usual answer is a proxy: one address with swappable code behind it, which leaves you a bookmark whose meaning can change after you audited it. zSwap splits the difference instead. Versions are immutable. Names move.

The lineage

Every version carries two frozen pointers: PREVIOUS(), set at construction, and successor(), write-once. The DAO ships a build by calling deployNext(initcode, salt) on the current tip, which CREATE2-deploys the successor and reverts unless that successor names the tip as its predecessor.

Beyond those pointers a successor can be anything: a different chunk count, a different reassembly, a different page. The chain's shape is the frozen part, since every outside reader depends on it. generation() counts back to the root and latest() follows successors forward, both walked rather than stored, because a stored counter is a second copy of what the pointers already say. Each walk stops at 32 hops, so a long chain returns an underestimate the caller can resume from instead of running out of gas.

The three-day wait

A tip is not served the moment it exists. zSwapResolver walks to the true tip, repeating the bounded latest() call from wherever the last answer landed, up to 256 versions. Then it steps back over anything younger than MATURITY, three days.

Age is read one link behind the tip: a version's birth time is written by its predecessor into succeededAt, never claimed by the version itself. Ship several builds in a burst and the walk-back passes through all the young ones to the newest that has stood long enough. That wait is what a stolen governance key runs into. It cannot move the name in one block; it has to survive three days of public view first.

What a reader can see

versions() lists the chain from the root, up to 64 entries, including a tip too young to be served; current() returns what is being served today. A picker should show both, since hiding the version a name is waiting on is the same staleness in another place. A build that has been superseded says so itself: the footer grows a newer → link, pointing at the tip under the same gateway when you arrived by address, and at w4eth.io otherwise.

html() never forwards to a successor. Forwarding would have been the smaller change and would have cost the property the design exists for: an address whose bytes cannot move under an auditor, a bookmark, or a gateway cache that was told the answer is immutable. Point a gateway at a version and you get that version, permanently. Point it at the name and you get whatever is current and mature.

Standards

ERC-8244 and the browsing path

That leaves the last step: turning an address into something a browser will open. Four standards cover it, and zSwap answers all of them, so it loads on whichever gateway or wallet a reader already has.

ERC-8244A contract with an html() function is a web page. The gateway calls html(), receives a string, serves it as text/html, and that is the entire standard. A gateway needs to know nothing about zSwap in particular, and the dapp implements nothing beyond the function it already had.
ERC-5219A fuller request interface. request(resource, params) answers with a status code, a body and headers; zSwap returns 200, the page, and its Content-Type and Cache-Control headers. Paths and query strings are ignored. It is one page, served from any URL under its address.
ERC-4804The web3:// scheme itself. resolveMode() returns "5219", which tells a client to use the interface above instead of guessing at calldata.
ERC-6821Name to contract. A gateway reads the contentcontract text record and falls back to the address record; zSwapResolver answers both, so either path arrives at the same version.

Four ways to open it

  • An ERC-8244 gateway: https://<address>.w4eth.io/
  • An ERC-4804 gateway: https://<address>.1.w3link.io/
  • A wallet or browser extension that speaks web3:// natively
  • By name, at zswap.wei.limo, which follows the resolver to whichever build is current and mature

zSwapResolver stores no address

The name is a .wei name in WNS, our own registry at 0x0000000000696760E15f265e828DB644A0c242EB. A .wei name is an NFT, and WNS resolves it to an address: id, then resolve. The resolver also answers the ENS interface, addr(bytes32), ENSIP-9 coin type 60 and text, so gateways written for either system land in the same place. The node argument is ignored, since the resolver is set on one name and answers for that name.

Normally a name has to be re-pointed after every upgrade, and that second transaction is the one people forget. Then the chain says v0.3 while the name still serves v0.1. This resolver stores no address, deriving the answer from the lineage walk above, so deployNext is the name update as well. There is no contenthash at all: the resolver does not implement one and does not claim to under ERC-165, because a contenthash names a blob and there is no blob here. text answers only contentcontract, and returns empty for any other key. The DAO's only power here is manage, a gated call into WNS and nowhere else, so the name stays renewable and re-pointable.

It is also a page in its own right, implementing html() and request() and relaying whatever version is current. A gateway can point at the resolver's own address and get the live dapp. The difference is the header: a version answers immutable, the resolver answers public, max-age=300, because a permanent cache hint on a moving answer is the failure it exists to prevent.

Reproducing one yourself. The salts and creation payloads are in the repo under deploy/, and node script/check-create2-artifacts.mjs re-derives every address from the committed source. One trap if you verify by hand: several contracts pin their optimizer run count because they do not otherwise fit EIP-170, Dutchboard at 20 and Swapboard, Floorboard and SwapboardView at 200, with the Precision suite verified at 200. A salt is only valid for initcode built at the pinned setting, so forge verify-contract needs --num-of-optimizations given explicitly.
